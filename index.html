How the plumbing works, in 60 seconds

  1. The Fed creates reserves. When the Fed buys a Treasury bill, it pays by crediting a bank's account at the Fed. That balance is a "reserve." Reserves are the cash banks use to pay each other, and only the Fed can create them.
  2. Two accounts pull cash out of the system. The Treasury keeps its checking account (the TGA) at the Fed; when taxes come in or it sells more debt, money leaves banks and sits there. Money funds can also park cash at the Fed overnight (reverse repo). Both shrink bank reserves.
  3. Scarce reserves show up as higher overnight rates. If banks have plenty of reserves, they lend cash overnight cheaply. If reserves get tight, overnight lending rates (SOFR, fed funds) push up toward the top of the Fed's range and can break through it. That's what happened in September 2019: on Sep 17 overnight repo rates touched 10% during the day, SOFR hit 5.25%, and fed funds rose to 2.30%, above the Fed's 2.00–2.25% range.
  4. The Fed has two fixes. It can lend cash on demand through its standing repo facility, which is meant to cap the spike. The cap is soft: SOFR was 4.22% on Oct 31, 2025, above the facility's 4.00% rate. Or it can buy Treasury bills to add reserves permanently. It began doing that in December 2025.

Why it matters for markets: when reserves are ample, this system is invisible. When they run short, funding costs jump, banks pull back from lending and trading, and the Fed is forced to step in. The page below tracks how close the system is to that edge.

Balance sheet and reserves

How much cash sits in the banking system, and what drains it.

Think of bank reserves as the water level in a tank. The Fed's asset purchases fill it. The Treasury's account and the reverse repo facility are two drains. Net liquidity combines all three into one number. These update weekly from the Fed's H.4.1 report, released Thursdays at 4:30pm ET (usually the next business day if Thursday is a holiday).

The rate corridor

  • The shaded band is the Fed's target range for overnight rates.
  • The Fed enforces it with three administered rates: the reverse repo floor, IORB in the middle, and the standing repo ceiling.
  • The dots are market rates. Sitting just under IORB is healthy. Dots rising toward the red ceiling would mean cash is getting scarce.

The Fed keeps overnight rates inside its target range using IORB, the standing repo ceiling and the reverse repo floor. Market rates (SOFR, EFFR) drifting up toward or above IORB is the classic sign reserves are getting scarce.

Money markets and stance

Where stress would show up first.

The balance sheet numbers above tell you how much cash is in the system. These tell you whether it's enough. If reserves were getting scarce, you'd see it here first: overnight rates creeping above what the Fed pays on reserves, and banks tapping the Fed's lending tools. The last tile measures the Fed's policy stance, the other lever it controls.

Net liquidity: Fed assets minus the Treasury's cash and reverse repo

  • A rough gauge of the cash sloshing through markets. Some traders watch it, arguing it has tracked risk assets like stocks and crypto over some periods. That link is loose and not guaranteed.
  • The level matters less than the direction. A steady slide means less of the Fed's cash is left in the system after the Treasury's account and reverse repo take their share; a rebound means more. Whether that moves stock or crypto prices is debated.

Glossary

Reserves
Cash banks hold in their accounts at the Fed. The base layer of the financial system; only the Fed creates it.
IORB (interest on reserve balances)
The rate the Fed pays banks on reserves. Banks have little reason to lend cash for less, so it anchors overnight rates. Lenders that can't earn it, such as money funds, often lend a little below it.
TGA (Treasury General Account)
The U.S. government's checking account at the Fed. Money in it is outside the banking system.
Overnight reverse repo (ON RRP)
A Fed facility where eligible lenders (money market funds, government-sponsored enterprises and large banks) lend cash to the Fed overnight. They can earn its rate risk-free, so they have little reason to lend for less. That puts a floor under overnight rates. It also acts as a reservoir of spare cash.
Repo
A short loan backed by Treasury securities as collateral. The main way Wall Street funds itself overnight.
SOFR (Secured Overnight Financing Rate)
A broad measure of the cost of overnight repo loans backed by Treasuries, based on about $3T of trades a day in October 2026 (New York Fed). The benchmark for most floating-rate loans.
EFFR (effective fed funds rate)
The typical rate on unsecured overnight loans in the fed funds market. Federal Home Loan Banks supplied about 97% of that lending (Fed note, 2024), mostly to U.S. branches of foreign banks. The rate the Fed officially targets.
Standing repo facility (SRF)
The Fed lends cash against Treasuries and other high-quality bonds to primary dealers and other approved counterparties, including banks, at a fixed rate (shown in the strip at the top). It is meant to limit how high repo rates can spike. The limit is soft: SOFR was 4.22% on Oct 31, 2025, above the 4.00% facility rate, and some dealers avoid it because of stigma.
QT (quantitative tightening)
Letting bonds mature without replacing them, which shrinks the balance sheet and drains reserves. The Fed ended QT on Dec 1, 2025, and started bill purchases later that month.
Reserve management purchases
Fed purchases of short-term bills to keep reserves ample. A technical fix to the plumbing, not stimulus like QE.
H.4.1
The Fed's weekly balance sheet report, released Thursdays at 4:30pm ET (usually the next business day if Thursday is a holiday). The source for most numbers on this page.
Ample reserves
The Fed's goal: enough reserves that overnight rates stay controlled by its administered rates, not by day-to-day swings in supply.

Deep dive: why SOFR versus IORB is the number to watch

IORB is what the Fed pays banks on cash left at the Fed. SOFR is what the market pays to borrow cash overnight against Treasuries. The gap between them does nothing mechanical by itself. It is a signal of how plentiful cash is, and the signal comes from who is doing the lending.

Green is anywhere under IORB. Amber starts at IORB. Red starts 5bp above it and runs to the standing repo ceiling. The markers are the latest market rates.

The two rates

IORB: set by the FedA fixed rate the Fed chooses. Only banks can earn it. It is risk-free and takes no effort: the cash just sits in the bank's account at the Fed.
SOFR: set by the marketThe going price of an overnight loan backed by Treasuries, based on trillions of dollars of trades a day. Banks, money market funds, dealers and hedge funds all trade in this market.

Why comparing them works

  • A bank can always earn IORB by doing nothing. So a bank has little reason to lend its cash overnight for less than that.
  • Money market funds and similar lenders cannot earn IORB. When cash is plentiful they compete to lend it and accept a little less. That pulls SOFR a few basis points under IORB.
  • When SOFR climbs to IORB or above on ordinary days, those lenders have run out of spare cash. Borrowers now have to pay enough to pull reserves out of banks. That is the sign reserves are no longer plentiful.

How to read the gap

A few bp below AmpleCash is plentiful and the Fed's rates are steering the market. This is the target state. The Fed calls it "ample reserves."
Level, up to 5bp above TighteningSpare cash is used up at the margin. Normal for a day or two around quarter-end or tax dates. A warning if it lingers on ordinary days.
5bp or more above StressedCash is scarce. Borrowers pay up, dealers turn to the Fed's standing repo facility, and rates get jumpy. September 2019 was the extreme case.

Lower is not always better. SOFR stuck down near the reverse repo floor would mean the system holds far more cash than it needs, so the Fed's balance sheet is bigger than necessary. The sweet spot is slightly under IORB.

What happens if SOFR stays above IORB

  • Borrowing costs rise a little for anyone who funds Treasuries overnight, such as dealers and hedge funds, and for loans priced off SOFR.
  • Fed funds usually follows. It is a smaller, slower market, so SOFR is the early warning and the fed funds tile is the confirmation.
  • The Fed is pushed to add reserves. The permanent fix is buying Treasury bills. The stopgap is lending through the standing repo facility.

Noise or signal? A quick test

  • Check the calendar. Quarter-ends, month-ends, mid-month tax dates and big Treasury debt settlement days all push SOFR up for a day or two. That is noise.
  • Check the next few days. If SOFR drops back under IORB once the date passes, nothing has changed. If it stays at or above IORB on ordinary days, that is signal.
  • Check the backstop. SOFR above IORB together with rising standing repo facility usage means dealers cannot find cash in the market. That is stress.

How the colors work. Each measure has fixed thresholds on its gauge. Green means liquidity is ample and markets are functioning normally; amber means the system is tightening or a buffer is thin; red means signs of reserve scarcity or funding stress. Reserve thresholds reference about $2.85T, close to where reserves were ($2.86T, week of Dec 3, 2025) just before the Fed started buying bills. Thresholds are judgment calls, not model outputs.

Net liquidity follows the common market convention: total Fed assets on Wednesday (H.4.1 Table 5), minus the Treasury's account as a week average, minus the reverse repo "Others" line on Wednesday (H.4.1 Table 1). It is not an official measure.
